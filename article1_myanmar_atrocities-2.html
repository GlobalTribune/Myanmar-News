Blood on All Sides: Massacres, Burned Villages and a War With No Innocents at Its Helm

As Myanmar's civil war enters its most brutal phase, investigative journalists document systematic atrocities committed by the military junta, resistance factions, and ethnic armed organisations alike — leaving civilians trapped between forces that claim to fight in their name.

James Alcott & Priya Suwan· Global Tribune Conflict Desk· Chiang Mai / Mae Sot· 10 May 2026

The dirt road into Thar Yar Kone village smells of ash. What remains of the settlement — seventeen homes, a monastery, a hand-pump well — is a scatter of scorched timber and corrugated iron, collapsed like broken ribs across the red earth. Twelve bodies were recovered here by local volunteers on the morning of 3 May. The perpetrators remain disputed.

That uncertainty is itself the story. In a war that began with a coup and has since fractured into a labyrinth of competing armed groups, the line between liberator and oppressor has dissolved. International journalists who spent three weeks embedded across three separate conflict zones in Shan and Kayah states found evidence of mass executions, deliberate targeting of aid convoys, and systematic torture — carried out not only by Myanmar's ruling military council, the State Administration Council (SAC), but by elements within the People's Defence Forces (PDF) and several prominent ethnic resistance organisations.

"We have documented summary executions by at least four distinct armed factions in the past sixty days alone. This is not a war with a good side." — Dr. Ananya Promchote, Human Rights Watch Field Director, Thailand

In the township of Loikaw, witnesses described SAC troops forcing families into a school compound before setting fire to the perimeter fence. Seventeen people died, according to testimony compiled by the Assistance Association for Political Prisoners. The junta has not responded to requests for comment.

But forty kilometres east, in a region nominally under PDF control, residents spoke of a different terror. A village headman — who asked not to be named, fearing reprisal — described resistance fighters executing three men accused of being military informants. No trial was held. Two of the men had brothers serving in the SAC; the third, he said, had simply borrowed money from the wrong person.

The Shan State Army – South, long regarded as one of the more disciplined ethnic armed organisations, has also faced credible allegations. Three aid workers from Médecins Sans Frontières described being detained for nine hours at an SSA-S checkpoint, during which their medical supplies were confiscated and one worker was beaten. The organisation filed a formal complaint; it was acknowledged and then, they say, ignored.

The United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs estimates that 1.9 million people are currently displaced across Myanmar. Access for international aid organisations remains critically restricted — not solely by the junta, but across frontlines. "We cannot reach people," said one OCHA coordinator, speaking on background. "There is no safe corridor. Every side uses humanitarian access as a bargaining chip."

"There is no humanitarian space left. They have turned the suffering of civilians into a weapon." — OCHA Field Coordinator (name withheld for security)

Survivors interviewed for this report did not ask which faction to support. They asked when it would stop. An elderly woman in a displacement camp outside Taunggyi, who lost her son to SAC shelling and her daughter-in-law to a PDF checkpoint shooting in the same month, looked at this correspondent with something past exhaustion. "Everyone," she said, "has a gun and a reason. None of them have a heart."

The international community has thus far responded with statements. Whether that changes may depend on what happens in the coming days in Washington.
